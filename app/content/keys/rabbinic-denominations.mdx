## 1. What “Rabbinic” Means

<Slide sources={["Rabbinic Judaism (overview)","https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbinic_Judaism"]}>
  <div>
    ### From Priests to Rabbis
    In the early Hebrewite era, religious authority centered on priests and the literal laws of the Bible.

    After the destruction of the Second Temple (70 AD), the priest-centered system was replaced by rabbis as teachers—laying the groundwork for what becomes known as “Rabbinic Judaism.”
  </div>

<Slide>
  <div>
    ### Torah as “Instruction”
    In this Rabbinic framework, “Torah” is not only the Bible as a document. It is treated as instruction that can include:
    - the Bible
    - the Babylonian Talmud
    - and the secret books traditionally associated with Kabbalah (such as the Zohar)
  </div>

## 2. The Four Denominations

<Slide>
  <div>
    ### Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, Reconstructionist
    The PDF summarizes four prominent approaches and how they relate to Kabbalah:

    * <b>Orthodox</b>: high engagement with Kabbalah as an esoteric “soul” layer, with strong emphasis on secrecy rules.
    * <b>Conservative</b>: treats tradition as living, and Kabbalah more as part of history and meaning than as practical magic.
    * <b>Reform</b>: emphasizes ethics and social justice; Kabbalah influence is described as lower and often reframed through public slogans.
    * <b>Reconstructionist</b>: frames Judaism as evolving civilization; Kabbalah is approached as study (myth/story) rather than secret power.
  </div>

    ### Elite Secrecy vs. Public “Garments”
    A key contrast is presented in the PDF: Orthodox sectors are described as still treating secrecy rules as literal obligations, while other branches are portrayed as focusing on the “garments” (culture and ethics) rather than the “soul” (secret spiritual machinery).
  </div>
